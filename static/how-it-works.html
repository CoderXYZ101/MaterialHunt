Method & accuracy

Last updated: 24 November 2026

MaterialHunt is a preliminary screening tool. It helps you narrow down which metals to look at. It does not replace an accredited laboratory test, a supplier certificate or an engineer's sign-off. This page shows what each part does, what it was checked against, and where it is weak.

Which model is used when (yield strength in the Simulator)

  1. Your mix matches a library grade (total difference within 6 weight-percent): the grade's typical datasheet yield. This is a table value, not machine learning.
  2. Otherwise, an iron-based alloyed steel inside the steel model's range (at least 40 atomic-% iron, only elements the model knows, and close to the 312 steels it learned from, whose yield strengths run from about 1,000 to 2,500 MPa): the steel model (extra-trees, 80 trees, trained on 312 measured steels).
  3. Otherwise, a mix inside the library model's range (close to compositions among the 48 library grades and the 312 steels): the library model (random forest, 80 trees, trained on the 48 library grades plus the 312 steels). A rough guide.
  4. Otherwise (outside both models' ranges, or the Estimator switch is on Rules): no ML estimate is made. The rough composition formula is used (average error about 105%) and the verdict is marked low reliability.

If you switch to My ML, the model you trained replaces steps 2 and 3 for the property it predicts.

The machine-learning part is small and its accuracy is measured and published below. Older versions of the site showed benchmark figures from a research dataset that did not describe this tool; those pages have been removed.

Cost

For a mix that matches a library grade, the simulator uses the middle of that grade's indicative stockist price range. For any other mix it adds up the raw-element prices, which is not the price of a finished bar or plate and can be off by a factor of two or more (the Evaluation page measures it). Always ask a supplier for a real quote.

How the verdict is calculated

The verdict uses simple screening formulas. It is not a finite-element or code-compliance calculation.

  • Strength: the matched library grade's typical yield, or an estimate (formula, or the ML model if you choose it).
  • Working strength = strength × temperature factor × environment factor × load-profile factor. The temperature factor falls as you approach the alloy's rough service limit; the other two are fixed multipliers (for example seawater or cyclic loading lower it).
  • Applied stress = the load you enter × the safety factor you choose (1 to 3).
  • Utilisation = applied stress ÷ working strength. Above 1.0 the verdict is UNSAFE; above 0.85 it is MARGINAL; otherwise SAFE.
  • Pressure: total pressure = static pressure + ½ × fluid density × speed². The "burst ceiling" is a fixed 5 GPa scale reduced by temperature, not a hoop-stress calculation from a wall thickness, so it only flags absurd pressures.
  • Worked example: 6061-T6 (yield 276 MPa) at 25 °C in air, 150 MPa load, safety factor 1.5 gives applied stress 225 MPa; with a working strength near 276 MPa, utilisation is about 0.82, so SAFE but close to the 0.85 marginal line.
  • Not modelled: geometry, wall thickness, stress concentrations, welds, fatigue S-N data, creep life, corrosion rates and manufacturing defects. Use it to rank options before a real calculation or test, not to certify a part.

3. Data sources

  • Grade library (48 grades): typical composition, strength, temperature rating, density and Indian price range for each grade, with ASTM, IS and EN designations. Entered from common standards and datasheets. Prices are indicative ranges and not quotes.
  • Element data (118 elements): density, atomic radius and indicative raw cost per element.
  • Failure rules: standard materials-engineering rules of thumb (for example chloride pitting, fatigue, BCC cold brittleness).

4. Limits you should know

  • It is a screening tool. Critical parts need an engineer and an accredited laboratory (for example NABL).
  • Strength in the simulator is a rough estimate. Use grade values for planning.
  • Prices are indicative ranges and change; supplier listings are for information only.
  • The grade library has 48 grades and does not cover every alloy, heat treatment or product form.
  • Some effects are not modelled (fatigue life, creep life, fracture toughness, specific standards). For creep the simulator only shows a warning when your temperature is high enough for it to matter; it does not predict how long a part will last. The Alloy Finder tells you when your request includes things it cannot check.
  • The lattice pictures and 3D shapes are for representation only.
  • The photo check is a basic heuristic and can be wrong.

5. A hypothetical business case (assumptions, not measured savings)

Nothing in this section is a measured saving. It shows how the tool's arithmetic works, so you can replace the assumptions with your own numbers.

A. Cost-Down Scanner on an example parts list

Example list (built into the tool): drone motor mount (titanium, 2 kg), landing gear leg (7075, 3.5 kg), gearbox housing (Inconel 625, 4 kg), fuel-line bracket (316L, 1.2 kg), chassis plate (6061, 6 kg). With the tool's default assumptions (dry, room temperature, stated or assumed loads, price = middle of the indicative range, safety factor target 1.5) it suggests cheaper grades for four of the five parts. The arithmetic gives a large "paper saving" for this list.

Why not to quote that number as a result: the assumptions are generous (room temperature and a dry environment, assumed loads on three parts, mid-range prices, ideal batch sizes). A real engineer would check temperature, corrosion, fatigue and manufacturing for each swap, and many would be rejected. The tool says the same: "Validate every change with your own stress analysis and a supplier quote."

6. What would make the validation stronger

  • Test real coupons of a few grades at an accredited lab and compare them with the numbers shown here.
  • Compare the strength estimate with a larger public dataset, and publish the errors.
  • Replace the strength estimate with grade-based values wherever a grade is matched.
  • Train and test an image model on real photos of damaged metal, and publish its accuracy.
